01 · style fingerprint

Where the draft's style sits versus every earlier plan

Each bar is one style measurement. The first two views express it as a z-score — against the eight earlier strategic plans, or against all 45 earlier documents; red: |z| > 2, outside anything the committee has published before. The third view is the pairwise comparison with SP-2023, the plan this draft replaces, shown as a ratio, because a z-score is undefined against a single document. The draft's prose is the most obligation-laden ("should" ×7.8 versus SP-2023) and uses the longest, most polysyllabic words in the record; on grade-level readability it is as dense as the 2017 and 2023 plans rather than denser.

Where the register hits its limit. Appendix C (pp. 270–271) is the draft's highest-grade section — Flesch–Kincaid 30.6 against the document's 17.9 — and the mechanism is a formatting one: it is a bulleted list with the bullets removed. Its clinical-protocol block runs 373 words in 5 sentences, with 58 semicolon-delimited items, a longest sentence of 121 words, and 1.1 finite verbs per 100 words against 3.6 across the draft. There are 26 such blocks in the draft, 1,790 words in all (2.1% of the text, 207 enumerated items) — against 3 blocks in SP-2023. Two things follow: setting them as numbered lists is the cheapest fix in the document, and removing all of them changes the draft's grade level by 0.1, so the readability result is about the main prose rather than the appendices.

03 · the document's own subject

The draft names autism less than half as often as any plan before it

Which label a document prefers — "people with autism", "autistic people", "on the spectrum" — changes with the field, and person-first usage has been falling across autism writing for a decade by deliberate community preference. So count them all together instead: every explicit reference to autism or to autistic people, in one pass, whichever wording is used. That total sat in a band of 199–314 per 10,000 words for seventeen years (mean 268, sd 33). The draft is at 108 — 4.8 standard deviations below the series mean, 2.6× below SP-2023 and 1.8× below even the lowest earlier plan (SP-2013, 199 per 10k). Both halves of the total fall: references to autistic people drop from 113 to 28 per 10k, and autism-as-a-topic mentions from 164 to 81. The identity-first "share" rises only because the denominator collapsed.

Meanwhile the names that fill the space are federal ones. Every plan from 2009 to 2023 named autism between 8× and 36× more often than it named a federal agency (agency acronyms 8–32 per 10k). The draft carries 167 agency acronyms per 10k and names agencies 1.5× more often than it names autism at all.

04 · trend reversals

Fifty-five of eighty trends reversed — in a corpus where reversing half is normal

Every content word with a strong monotonic trend across 2007–2023 (|Spearman ρ| ≥ 0.5), and whether the draft continues or reverses it, judged against the average of the 2018-onwards documents. Read this section with its control. The control applies the draft's own rule to every earlier document against its own past — trends refitted on the documents published before it, judged against its own trailing eight-year window — because a document that predates a fixed recent window sits on the reversing side of any time trend by construction, and scoring it that way would flatter the draft. On that like-for-like basis the 28 earlier documents reverse 25–76% of their own trend sets (median 48%): a reversal share near half is the corpus norm, and this measure has no minimum size of change. The draft's 69% is second of 29 documents and the highest of the strategic plans on that measure — SP-2013 25%, SP-2019 42%, SP-2023 48%, SP-2017 61% — but that version has no magnitude floor and mixes document types, and the stricter pairwise test below does not agree with it.

The pairwise check, with no window and no baseline choice. Comparing each plan only with the plan immediately before it, on one fixed vocabulary of 386 terms: the draft changes 62.2% of it by a factor of two or more — the largest step in the series, against 50.5% for 2019-vs-2017 and 13–35% across the annual-update era — with 45.1% of terms at least halved and the median term at 0.60× its SP-2023 rate. That is the rupture finding in its strongest form, because it needs no trend fit and no baseline. On the strict pairwise reversal rule, though — plan-series trend, judged against the predecessor's own level, with a 2× magnitude floor — the draft reverses 32.4% and SP-2023 reverses 42.8%: SP-2023 was an expansionary rewrite whose increases reversed falling trends, while the draft mostly pushes already-falling terms further down. The draft is the most divergent plan-to-plan step in the record, not the most trend-reversing one. Full table in consecutive_plan_change.csv.

red = the 2026 draft · each document scored by the draft's rule against its own past · the per-term working for the retrospective diagnostic (876 judgements across 11 documents) is in reversal_control_by_term.csv, and the walk-forward figures in trend_reversal_walkforward.csv · no term is reversed by the draft alone, and 61 of the 80 are reversed by 8 or more documents — the measure is noisy, which is why the 13 substantial reversals below are the ones to quote.

The substantial reversals view applies a floor: the term was used at least 5 times per 10,000 words in the 2018+ documents and the draft at least halves it. Thirteen terms clear it — including health, disparities, adulthood, racial, suicide, sex and justice.

07 · institutional memory

Almost nothing carried forward

Share of each plan's 8-word sequences already present in earlier IACC publications. The draft inherits 0.03% from the record and 0.02% from SP-2023. The 24% average often quoted for earlier plans is carried by the annual-update era — SP-2010 inherited 53%, SP-2013 40% — when each plan was a revision of the last. The like-for-like comparison is the recent one: SP-2023 took 0.94% of its text from SP-2019 and SP-2017 took 1.1% from SP-2013, so modern plans were already near-clean-sheet documents. The draft is roughly 50× below that recent norm, not 800× below the historical one. Its preface offers a partial explanation:

“Our drafting process did not have the benefit of a complete institutional record or formal handoff from the prior planning cycle.”

09 · methods & caveats

How the numbers are made

Rates are normalised per 1,000 or 10,000 words so documents of different lengths compare fairly. Readability uses Flesch/Flesch–Kincaid plus four corroborating formulas. Lexical diversity is MATTR-500. The deontic index is the share of obligation modals (must/shall/should/required-to) among all modal verbs. Nominalisation counts words ending -tion/-ment/-ness/-ity — verbs frozen into nouns. Keyness is the weighted log-odds ratio with an informative Dirichlet prior. Text reuse counts shared 8-word shingles. Trends are Spearman correlations of term rates with publication year.

Sentence splitting, syllable counting, and passive detection are heuristic but applied identically to every document. Reference lists, running headers/footers, and tables of contents were removed before measuring; easy-read/at-a-glance editions and documents under 2,000 words were excluded (15 of the 61 inventoried publications). Everything re-runs from the shipped text cache in the repo, no PDFs required.

Which comparator. This page defaults to SP-2023, the plan the draft replaces. Averaging the eight plans from 2009 to 2023 overstates several changes, because SP-2023 had already made part of the move: "ASD" had fallen from a 157-per-10k plan average to 21 by 2023 before the draft took it to 1.2, and person-first naming from 55 to 19 before the draft took it to 2.6. On one measure the comparator flips the sign — identity-first naming is above the eight-plan average but 2.7× below SP-2023, which is why this page reports the combined subject-reference total in section 03 rather than any single naming convention. Z-scores against eight stylistically consistent plans have small denominators and inflated magnitudes (a z of +31 means "far outside the range", not a precise distance); read direction from the z views and size from the SP-2023 view.

Readability. Six formulas are computed. The draft is the most extreme in the record on Flesch Reading Ease (0.6) and Coleman–Liau, both of which weight word length heavily; on Flesch–Kincaid grade, Gunning Fog, SMOG and ARI it ranks second or third, behind SP-2023, because its sentences are shorter (19.2 words vs 28.4). The claim this page makes is therefore the word-length one — 49% of its words run 7+ letters against 41% in SP-2023, the highest in the record — and not that it is the least readable document by every measure. FRE below about 10 is an extrapolation past the bottom of a scale calibrated on schoolbook prose; it is reported for continuity with the other documents, not as a precise reading level.

What this analysis cannot show. Intent. A genre change, a jargon-dense register and near-zero inherited text are equally consistent with a committee working without a handoff under deadline — which the draft's preface states — as with a deliberate break. The document is also a working draft issued for public comment and marked "subject to errors and revision"; the published plans it is measured against are finished products, which matters for anything downstream of editing and polish, including the absence of a plain-language companion.
